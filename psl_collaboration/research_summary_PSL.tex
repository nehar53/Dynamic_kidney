{\Large\bfseries Fairness over time in dynamic kidney exchange: a research summary}\\[2pt]
{\small Dr Yousef Amer (Adelaide University, Australia) and Neha (Manipal University Jaipur, India).
Prepared for potential collaborators.}

\section*{1. Overview}
In a kidney exchange program, patients with a willing but incompatible living donor
swap donors with other pairs, through short cycles or through chains started by an
altruistic donor. The pool is cleared again and again. After each clearing, matched
pairs leave, the rest keep waiting, some withdraw, some receive a deceased-donor
kidney, and new pairs join. Most studies of fairness, however, look at a single
clearing round.

We study this question for the Australian and New Zealand Paired Kidney Exchange
(ANZKX). Public reports give 60--90 registered pairs at any time and 50--66 transplants
per year, with about 30\% of patients highly sensitized and therefore hard to match.
Using this information, we built a synthetic simulator and a multi-objective framework
that balances the number of transplants against three fairness criteria. We ask two
things: how the price of fairness changes from one clearing round to the next, and
whether clearing every month or waiting for a larger pool better preserves the balance
between efficiency and equity. A paper on the static version of the problem is under
review; a manuscript on the dynamic version is in preparation.

\textbf{All results below come from synthetic simulation informed by public ANZKX
information. They are not clinical results and have not been validated on patient-level
data.}

\section*{3. Model and methods}
\textbf{Pool dynamics.} In each round $t$ the pool changes as
$V_{t+1} = (V_t \setminus M_t \setminus X_t \setminus W_t) \cup A_t$, where $M_t$ are
matched pairs, $X_t$ patients who leave for a deceased-donor transplant, $W_t$ patients
who withdraw after waiting $\tau$ months, and $A_t$ new arrivals
($|A_t| \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda)$). Two vertices are compatible if blood type, a
cPRA-based virtual crossmatch and the transport time between their nodes all allow it.
Cycles contain at most 3 pairs and chains at most 4 transplants. All methods see the
same patients in each run.

\textbf{Objectives.} Each round is a four-objective problem (Table~\ref{tab:obj}).
For a matching $M$, $P$ is the set of patients, $S$ the highly sensitized patients
(cPRA $\geq 0.80$), and $R$ and $U$ the patients at distant and central nodes.

\begin{table}[h]
\small
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}p{0.6cm}p{3.2cm}p{5.6cm}L@{}}
\toprule
 & Objective & Definition & Goal \\
\midrule
$F_1$ & Transplant volume & $|M \cap P|$ & Maximize \\
$F_2$ & Waiting-time equity & Gini coefficient of waiting times after the round & Minimize \\
$F_3$ & Access gap & $\bigl|\,|M \cap S|/|S| - |M \cap P|/|P|\,\bigr|$
      & Minimize: highly sensitized patients matched at the same rate as others \\
$F_4$ & Logistics gap & $\bigl|\,|M \cap R|/|R| - |M \cap U|/|U|\,\bigr|$
      & Minimize (a modeled proxy, not a validated measure) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\caption{Objectives solved in each clearing round.}
\label{tab:obj}
\end{table}

The \textbf{price of fairness} in round $t$ is the share of the maximum possible
transplants given up for fairness:
$\mathrm{PoF}_t = 100\,(F_1^{\mathrm{ILP}}(t) - F_1(t))/F_1^{\mathrm{ILP}}(t)$.

\textbf{Methods compared.} Table~\ref{tab:methods} lists the main methods. Ten
configurations in total were run, including static and batched variants.

\begin{table}[h]
\small
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}p{3.6cm}L@{}}
\toprule
Method & How it chooses a matching \\
\midrule
ILP-guided NSGA-III (our framework)
  & An integer linear program (ILP) finds the largest matching. This matching seeds
    NSGA-III, gets priority in the repair step and is kept in an archive, so the front
    always contains it. Small rounds ($\leq 200$ feasible matchings) are solved exactly.
    A bandit sets the mutation rate. The chosen matching keeps at least 90\% of the
    maximum volume and lies closest to the ideal point in $(F_2, F_3, F_4)$. \\
Volume ILP & The largest matching in each round. \\
Two-stage ILP (exact)
  & Fixes the largest volume $F_1^*$, then minimizes $F_2/r_2 + F_3/r_3 + F_4/r_4$,
    where $r_k$ is each objective's value in the volume ILP's matching: the fairest
    matching of maximum size. \\
Lexicographic rule & Most highly sensitized patients first, then most transplants. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\caption{Main methods compared.}
\label{tab:methods}
\end{table}

\textbf{Experimental design.} Three withdrawal thresholds, 100 paired runs each, paired
Wilcoxon tests with Bonferroni correction. The framework and the two-stage ILP are
compared at three levels: the same pool in one round, the same pool in every round of a
trajectory, and full trajectories in which each method shapes its own future pools.
Sensitivity, scalability and out-of-distribution tests on PrefLib dataset 00036 complete
the evaluation. The exit rate, withdrawal thresholds, transport times, the 24\,h CIT
limit and the HLA proxy are modeling assumptions, not validated ANZKX parameters. With
$\lambda = 10$ the simulator gives 65--70 transplants per year, a little above the
reported 50--66, so we also test $\lambda = 7$ and $\lambda = 13$.

\section*{4. Key findings}
Table~\ref{tab:findings} summarizes the results (simulation, 100 paired runs unless
stated otherwise).

\begin{table}[h]
\small
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}p{3.6cm}L@{}}
\toprule
Question & Result \\
\midrule
Does fairness cost transplants over time?
  & Barely. Clearing monthly, the framework transplanted as many patients as the
    volume ILP over 25 rounds, while reducing $F_3$ by 21--24\%, $F_4$ by 28--34\% and
    $F_2$ by 3--4\%. The price of fairness averaged 1.6--1.9\% per round (8.4\% in the
    first round, then flat), and 94--100\% of deferred transplants were made later. \\
Monthly or batched clearing?
  & Monthly. Waiting for 90 patients never triggered a clearing at $\tau = 6$, so 65.6
    highly sensitized patients withdrew per run against 25.5 under monthly clearing. At
    $\tau = 12$ and 18, batching lost 24\% and 12\% of transplants. \\
How does the exact two-stage ILP compare?
  & It is stronger at maximum volume. On 30 identical pools it reached $F_3 = 0.022$ and
    $F_4 = 0.035$, against 0.055 and 0.114 for the framework's maximum-volume choice,
    and over full trajectories it made 1.0--1.3 more transplants per run. \\
What does the evolutionary front add?
  & Options below maximum volume. Its solutions were close to the exact reference at
    each volume level (2.167 vs.\ 2.146 at 80\%, $\tau = 12$), and an 80\% floor lowered
    $F_3$ by 17--22\% relative to the two-stage ILP for 2.1--2.9\% fewer transplants.
    The floor rule did not always pick the fairest of these solutions. \\
What drives search quality?
  & ILP guidance. Without it, NSGA-III never reached the largest matching (0 of 30
    pools; 32.6 vs.\ 42.3 transplants) and the price of fairness was 28.3\%; with it,
    30 of 30 pools and 8.2\%. The bandit did not improve on the best fixed mutation rate. \\
What does each method cost?
  & Two-stage ILP: 5.08\,s per trajectory round, but 233.3\,s on 75-vertex pools (67\%
    of solves time-limited). Framework: 19.30\,s and 27.2\,s. The exact method returns
    one matching per volume level; the framework returns the whole front in one run. \\
Do the results carry over to PrefLib?
  & Partly. On 12 instances of 128--147 vertices (120 paired runs), volume matched the
    ILP (75.03 vs.\ 75.38), the fairness gain was small and not significant ($F_3$ 0.060
    vs.\ 0.062), and the two-stage ILP was again stronger at maximum volume. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\caption{Main findings.}
\label{tab:findings}
\end{table}

\textbf{What this means.} The framework does not outperform every baseline. For the
fairest matching at maximum volume, the two-stage ILP is the better tool. A
lexicographic rule gives highly sensitized patients more transplants, but at the cost of
a larger access gap. The framework is useful when some volume may be traded for
fairness, because it offers a range of solutions to choose from.

\textbf{Limitations.} The exact solves hit their time limits on 75-vertex pools, so
their solutions there are the best found rather than proven optima. The framework's
front is approximate, it could not be run at 500 vertices because of memory limits, and
its floor rule does not always select the fairest solution. $F_4$ is a proxy.

\section*{5. Open questions for collaboration}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}p{4.3cm}L@{}}
\toprule
Partner expertise & Possible joint work \\
\midrule
Market design, kidney exchange policy & Clearing and batching rules that use information
about departures (G4); comparison with national programs; theory to explain why monthly
clearing did better here. \\
Fair division, computational social choice & Axiomatic analysis of the fairness criteria
and the selection rule; group versus individual fairness; fairness defined over time
rather than per round (G5). \\
Multi-objective combinatorial optimization & Exact or approximate Pareto fronts (for
example $\varepsilon$-constraint ILPs) below maximum volume; selection rules that match
the exact objective; larger pools. \\
Healthcare operations, health economics & More realistic deceased-donor and logistics
models (G2, G3); the value of a transplant and of waiting time; a validation study with
program data, subject to ethics approval. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}

\section*{6. What we bring}
A dynamic simulator of a kidney exchange pool with operational constraints, informed by
public ANZKX information; exact ILP and two-stage ILP baselines; a tested
multi-objective pipeline with paired-run statistics; and a set of open questions between
optimization, market design and fairness.

We would welcome an exchange of ideas on these questions. If there is mutual interest,
this could begin with an online discussion and grow into joint work on one of them.
