{\Large\bfseries Fairness over time in dynamic kidney exchange: a research summary}\\[2pt]
{\small Dr Yousef Amer (Adelaide University, Australia) and Neha (Manipal University Jaipur, India).
Prepared for potential collaborators.}

\section*{1. Overview}
Our interest in this problem began with the Australian and New Zealand Paired Kidney
Exchange (ANZKX). The program matches patients who have a willing but incompatible
living donor, and it does so continuously: between 60 and 90 pairs are registered at
any time, the program performs 50--66 transplants a year, and roughly 30\% of patients
are highly sensitized, which makes them very hard to match. Patients who are not
matched in one round stay in the pool, and while they wait some of them withdraw and
others receive a deceased-donor kidney instead.

Most of the work on fairness in kidney exchange, including the price of fairness of
Dickerson et al., considers one clearing round at a time. That leaves open what happens
to fairness, and to its cost, when the same pool is cleared month after month. In our
earlier work (currently under review) we looked at a static ANZKX pool. The present
project moves to the dynamic setting. We built a synthetic simulator from public ANZKX
information and an optimization framework that weighs the number of transplants against
three fairness criteria, and we use them to ask how the price of fairness behaves across
clearing rounds and whether monthly or batched clearing keeps a better balance between
efficiency and equity. A manuscript on these results is in preparation.

\textbf{Every result in this summary comes from synthetic simulation informed by public
ANZKX information. None of it has been validated on patient-level data.}

\section*{3. Model}
In round $t$ the pool is updated as
$V_{t+1} = (V_t \setminus M_t \setminus X_t \setminus W_t) \cup A_t$. Here $M_t$ is the
set of matched pairs, $X_t$ the patients who leave for a deceased-donor transplant,
$W_t$ those who withdraw after $\tau$ months, and $A_t$ the new arrivals, whose number
is Poisson with mean $\lambda$. Whether a donor can give to a patient depends on blood
type, a virtual crossmatch driven by the patient's cPRA, and whether the kidney can
travel between the two hospitals within the CIT limit. We allow cycles of up to three
pairs and chains of up to four transplants, and every method is run on exactly the same
sequence of patients.

Each round is treated as a four-objective problem (Table~\ref{tab:obj}). In the
definitions, $P$ is the set of patients, $S \subseteq P$ the highly sensitized patients
(cPRA $\geq 0.80$), and $R$ and $U$ the patients registered at distant and at central
nodes.

\begin{table}[h]
\small
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}p{0.6cm}p{3.0cm}p{5.8cm}L@{}}
\toprule
 & Objective & Definition & Direction \\
\midrule
$F_1$ & Transplant volume & $|M \cap P|$ & maximize \\
$F_2$ & Waiting-time equity & Gini coefficient of waiting times after the round & minimize \\
$F_3$ & Access gap & $\bigl|\,|M \cap S|/|S| - |M \cap P|/|P|\,\bigr|$ & minimize \\
$F_4$ & Logistics gap & $\bigl|\,|M \cap R|/|R| - |M \cap U|/|U|\,\bigr|$
      & minimize (proxy only) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\caption{Objectives in each clearing round.}
\label{tab:obj}
\end{table}

We measure the price of fairness in round $t$ as
$\mathrm{PoF}_t = 100\,\bigl(F_1^{\mathrm{ILP}}(t) - F_1(t)\bigr)/F_1^{\mathrm{ILP}}(t)$,
where $F_1^{\mathrm{ILP}}(t)$ is the largest number of transplants possible in that round.

\section*{4. Methods}
Our framework uses NSGA-III, but plain NSGA-III turned out to miss the largest matching
in every one of 30 test pools, which would have inflated the price of fairness with
search error. We therefore solve an integer linear program (ILP) first and give its
matching a special role: it seeds the population, its cycles and chains are tried first
in the repair step, and an external archive keeps it, so the final front always contains
a matching of maximum size. When a round has at most 200 feasible matchings we skip the
search and enumerate them. A bandit (UCB1 or LinUCB) picks the mutation rate from the
hypervolume gain. From the front we implement the matching that keeps at least 90\% of
the maximum volume and lies closest to the ideal point in $(F_2, F_3, F_4)$; floors of
100\% and 80\% are tested as well.

To judge the framework fairly we also built an exact two-stage ILP. Stage 1 finds the
maximum volume $F_1^*$; stage 2 keeps $F_1 = F_1^*$ and minimizes
$F_2/r_2 + F_3/r_3 + F_4/r_4$, with each $r_k$ taken from the volume-maximizing
matching. It returns the fairest matching of maximum size and is a stronger baseline
than the volume ILP. A lexicographic rule that serves highly sensitized patients first
completes the main comparison; ten configurations were run in total.

Each configuration was run 100 times at each of the three withdrawal thresholds, with
paired runs and Wilcoxon tests (Bonferroni corrected). We compared the framework with
the two-stage ILP on identical single pools, on the same pools along a fixed
trajectory, and over full trajectories where each method shapes its own future pools.
We also ran sensitivity and scalability tests and an out-of-distribution test on
PrefLib dataset 00036. Several inputs are our own assumptions rather than validated
ANZKX parameters: the exit rate, the withdrawal thresholds, transport times, the 24\,h
CIT limit and the HLA proxy. With $\lambda = 10$ the simulator produces 65--70
transplants a year, a little more than ANZKX reports, so $\lambda = 7$ and $13$ were
tested too.

Two messages come out of these numbers. Over time, fairness turned out to be cheap:
most of the transplants given up in one round were made in a later one, and waiting for
a larger pool did not help. At the same time, the framework is not the best method in
every respect. If the goal is the fairest matching of maximum size, the exact two-stage
ILP does better, and a lexicographic rule gives highly sensitized patients more
transplants, although it opens a larger access gap. Where the framework helps is below
maximum volume, where its front offers trade-offs close to the exact ones; our own floor
rule, however, did not always pick the best of them.

There are also clear limits. On 75-vertex pools the exact solves reached their time
limits, so those solutions are the best found and not proven optima. The framework's
front is approximate, and we could not run it on 500-vertex pools because of memory.
$F_4$ is a proxy rather than a measure of geographic inequity.
