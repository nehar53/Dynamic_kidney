{\Large\bfseries Fairness in dynamic kidney exchange: a research summary}\\[2pt]
{\small Dr Yousef Amer (Adelaide University, Australia) and Neha (Manipal University Jaipur, India).
Prepared for potential collaborators at Universit\'e PSL. October 2026.}

\section*{1. Overview}
We study how a kidney exchange programme can match patients fairly over time
without giving up transplants. Patients with an incompatible living donor, and
altruistic donors, join a pool. Each month the programme selects cycles and
chains of transplants. Our setting is the Australian and New Zealand Paired
Kidney Exchange (ANZKX). From published aggregate statistics, ANZKX performs
about 50 transplants per year from 60--90 registered pairs, and about 30\% of
patients are highly sensitised (hard to match). We build a simulation of this
setting and an optimisation framework that balances transplant volume with three
fairness criteria. A related paper by the same team is under review. All results
below come from simulation. They are not clinical results and were not
validated on patient-level data.

\section*{2. Research gaps we address}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Fairness in kidney exchange is mostly studied within a single clearing
        round, not over the trajectory of a dynamic pool.
  \item Fairness is usually a single criterion (for example, priority for highly
        sensitised patients), not several criteria at once.
  \item Operational constraints such as exits to deceased-donor transplantation,
        withdrawals after long waits and cold ischaemia time (CIT) limits are often
        left out of the optimisation models.
\end{enumerate}

\section*{3. Our optimisation framework}
\textbf{Dynamic pool.} Over 25 monthly rounds, matched pairs leave, other patients
may leave for a deceased-donor transplant (rate $\mu_d$), patients who have waited
$\tau$ months withdraw ($\tau \in \{6, 12, 18\}$), and new pairs arrive (Poisson,
rate $\lambda = 10$). Compatibility uses blood type, a virtual crossmatch driven by
cPRA, and door-to-door transport time against a CIT limit between six regional
nodes. Cycles have 2--3 pairs; chains start at an altruist and have up to 4
transplants.\\
\textbf{Modelling assumptions (not validated ANZKX parameters):} $\mu_d = 0.02$,
the $\tau$ values, transport times and the 24\,h CIT limit, node weights, the HLA
mismatch proxy, and $\lambda = 10$. The calibration does not match ANZKX exactly
(about 65--70 simulated transplants per year against about 50).\\
\textbf{Objectives.} $F_1$: transplants (maximise). $F_2$: Gini coefficient of
waiting times after the round. $F_3$: gap between the match rate of highly
sensitised patients (cPRA $\ge 0.80$) and the overall match rate. $F_4$: gap between
the match rates of patients at distant and central nodes. $F_2$--$F_4$ are minimised.\\
\textbf{Exact anchor.} An integer linear program (ILP) gives the maximum number of
transplants $F_1^*$ in each round. A two-stage ILP then fixes $F_1 = F_1^*$ and
finds the fairest maximum matching (a weighted sum of $F_2$, $F_3$, $F_4$, each scaled by
its value in the volume-maximising matching). We use it as a benchmark.\\
\textbf{ILP-guided NSGA-III.} A many-objective evolutionary algorithm (NSGA-III)
searches for the Pareto front in each round. An exact ILP solution seeds the search
and receives priority in the repair step, and an external archive keeps every
non-dominated matching. Small rounds are solved exactly by enumeration. A bandit
(UCB1 or LinUCB) adapts the mutation rate. Two guidance variants are under
evaluation: guidance by the volume-maximising ILP and guidance by the two-stage ILP.
The choice between them is still under discussion.\\
\textbf{Selection rule.} From the front, the implemented matching must keep at least
90\% of $F_1^*$ (other floors are tested). The Price of Fairness (PoF) is the
percentage of $F_1^*$ given up in a round.\\
\textbf{Design.} All methods see the same pools, arrivals and exits in each run
(paired runs). We test with paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests and Bonferroni correction.

\section*{4. Key findings so far (simulation)}
Synthetic pools calibrated to ANZKX aggregate statistics, 100 paired runs per
setting, unless stated. Framework = volume-ILP-guided NSGA-III with the 90\% floor.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Myopic vs batched clearing.} Clearing every month beat waiting for
        90 patients at every $\tau$. Batched clearing never cleared at $\tau = 6$, and it
        made 24\% fewer transplants at $\tau = 12$ and 12\% fewer at $\tau = 18$. At
        $\tau = 6$, 65.6 highly sensitised patients withdrew under batched clearing against
        25.5 under myopic clearing. No batching variant we tested beat myopic clearing.
  \item \textbf{Fairness at almost no volume cost.} Against the volume-maximising
        ILP, total transplants over 25 rounds did not differ significantly. $F_3$ fell by
        21.4 / 24.4 / 24.1\% and $F_4$ by 28.1 / 34.0 / 32.3\% ($\tau$ = 6 / 12 / 18).
        Mean PoF was 1.60 / 1.81 / 1.91\%. It was 8.44\% in round 1 and flat afterwards.
        About 7.0--8.0 transplants per run were deferred round by round, and 94--100\%
        of them were made in later rounds.
  \item \textbf{Two-stage ILP benchmark.} At maximum volume, the exact two-stage ILP
        found fairer matchings than our framework. On 30 identical pools, it reached
        $F_3 = 0.0217$ and $F_4 = 0.0348$, against 0.0548 and 0.1143 for the framework's
        maximum-volume choice. Over every round of fixed trajectories, the framework
        achieved about 58\% of the possible improvement over the volume ILP. The value of
        the evolutionary front lies below maximum volume. For example, an 80\% floor gave
        a further 23\% reduction in $F_3$ for 1.8\% fewer transplants ($\tau = 12$).
  \item \textbf{Guidance matters.} Without ILP guidance, NSGA-III reached the
        volume optimum in 0 of 30 pools (32.6 against 42.3 transplants). With guidance it
        did so in 30 of 30.
  \item \textbf{PrefLib 00036} (12 real compatibility graphs, simulated arrivals,
        120 paired units). Transplants did not differ from the ILP (75.03 against 75.38),
        and the fairness gain was small and not significant ($F_3$ 0.060 against 0.062).
  \item \textbf{Adaptive operator selection: a negative result.} UCB1 and LinUCB gave
        the same outcomes, and dynamic and static versions did not differ. Under ILP
        guidance the bandit did not improve results.
\end{itemize}
The framework does not outperform every baseline. The two-stage ILP is stronger at
maximum volume, and a lexicographic rule that prioritises highly sensitised patients
gives more such transplants (with a larger $F_3$ gap).
